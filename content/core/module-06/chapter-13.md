## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:
- Explain the major uses of abstracts.
- Explain Current Awareness Services (CAS) and Selective Dissemination of Information (SDI).
- Describe how abstracts function within bibliographic databases and major indexing-and-abstracting services.
- Explain the difference between extractive and abstractive summarisation.
- Discuss the relationship between human and AI-assisted abstracting.

## 13.1 Abstracts in Databases

Bibliographic and indexing-and-abstracting databases typically contain titles, authors, publication details, assigned indexing terms, and abstracts. The abstract helps a user judge the relevance of a document before deciding whether to obtain the full text.

## 13.2 Current Awareness Services (CAS)

Current Awareness Services keep users informed about newly available information within a subject area of interest to them. Alerts sent through such services may include citations together with abstracts, allowing users to quickly identify newly published material that is relevant to their work.

## 13.3 Selective Dissemination of Information (SDI)

Selective Dissemination of Information distributes newly available material according to the individual interests or profile of each user. Abstracts help recipients of an SDI service decide quickly whether a newly identified document is actually useful to them.

## 13.4 Abstracts and Information Retrieval

A single search can return hundreds of records. Reading the abstracts attached to those records allows a user to screen them efficiently, rather than having to read every full document, and to select only the most promising ones for closer reading.

## 13.5 Major Indexing and Abstracting Services

Indexing and abstracting services provide organised access to scholarly and professional literature across disciplines, combining the indexing and abstracting principles covered throughout this guide into large-scale, continuously updated products. Well-known examples include Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed/MEDLINE, the ERIC database, Chemical Abstracts, Library and Information Science Abstracts (LISA), Library Literature & Information Science Full Text, and the EBSCO and ProQuest families of databases. These services assist researchers in discovering literature across different disciplines, and are themselves a practical demonstration of subject indexing and abstracting at scale.

## 13.6 Automated Summarisation

Automated summarisation uses computational methods to produce shorter representations of documents. It becomes especially useful when very large collections of material must be processed quickly, at a scale that would be impractical for human abstractors alone.

## 13.7 Extractive Summarisation

Extractive methods work by selecting important words, phrases, or whole sentences directly from the source document. The resulting summary is therefore built largely from text that already existed in the original.

## 13.8 Abstractive Summarisation

Abstractive methods attempt to represent the meaning of the source document using newly generated wording, rather than simply lifting text from the original. Modern AI systems can assist with this kind of summarisation, but their output may sometimes omit important details or distort the original meaning, so it always requires careful checking against the source.

## 13.9 Human Abstractors versus Automated Systems

Human abstractors bring contextual judgement to their work and can tailor an abstract for a specialised audience in a way that automated systems currently cannot match. Automated systems, in turn, offer speed and the ability to work at a much larger scale. In practice, a workflow that combines automated assistance with human quality control often produces the best results.

## 13.10 The Complete Workflow

The relationship between indexing and abstracting can be summarised as a single continuous workflow: a document undergoes subject analysis, is indexed, is abstracted, becomes part of a database record, is found through a user’s search, is screened by the user through its record, and is finally selected for full-text reading. Indexing provides the structured access points that make a document findable in the first place; abstracting then provides the concise representation of content that helps the user decide whether it is worth reading in full.
