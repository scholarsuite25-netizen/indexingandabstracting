## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:
- Define an abstract.
- Explain content extraction and editing.
- Distinguish between indicative, informative, critical, slant, author's, and structured abstracts.
- Write a simple academic abstract.

## 12.1 What Is an Abstract?

An abstract is a concise representation of a document’s content. Depending on its particular type, an abstract may present the purpose of a study, the method used, the main findings, and the conclusion reached.

## 12.2 Why Abstracts Matter

Abstracts help users screen large amounts of literature quickly and decide which documents genuinely deserve a full reading. They are particularly important within bibliographic and indexing-and-abstracting databases, where a user may be scanning hundreds of records at once.

## 12.3 Content Extraction

When preparing an abstract, the writer should ask a consistent set of questions of the source document:
- What is the problem being addressed?
- What is the objective of the work?
- What exactly was studied?
- How was it studied — what method was used?
- What were the main findings?
- What conclusion or recommendation was reached?

## 12.4 Principles of Good Abstracting

A good abstract should be clear, concise, accurate, objective, self-contained, coherent, grammatically correct, and faithful to the source document. It should never introduce unsupported claims, invented information, or the personal opinions of the person writing the abstract.

## 12.5 The Indicative Abstract

An indicative abstract tells the reader what a document covers and answers the question “What does this document discuss?”, but it usually does not report detailed findings. It is generally brief.

## EXAMPLE

This article discusses the application of artificial intelligence in university libraries, examining major uses, potential benefits, implementation challenges, and implications for future services.

## 12.6 The Informative Abstract

An informative abstract provides substantive information, most commonly including the purpose, method, findings, and conclusion of a study. It is detailed enough that a reader can often use it to judge whether the original document is worth reading in full.

## EXAMPLE

This study examined AI adoption among university librarians in Nigeria. A questionnaire was administered to librarians in selected universities. The findings showed high awareness but limited formal training, along with concerns about accuracy and data privacy. The study recommends regular AI-literacy programmes and institutional guidance.

## 12.7 The Indicative-Informative Abstract

As the name suggests, an indicative-informative abstract combines features of both types above: it indicates the general scope of the document while also reporting some of its substantive findings, without going into the full detail of a purely informative abstract.

## 12.8 The Critical Abstract

A critical abstract goes a step further than a purely descriptive summary. In addition to summarising the content, it offers an assessment or evaluation of the document — commenting, for example, on the strengths, weaknesses, methodology, reliability, or significance of the work. Because it involves judgement rather than pure description, a critical abstract is usually prepared by someone with subject expertise.

## 12.9 The Author's Abstract

An author’s abstract is simply an abstract prepared by the author of the original document, rather than by an independent indexer or abstractor. Many journals require authors to submit one alongside their article.

## 12.10 The Slant Abstract

A slant abstract emphasises the particular aspects of a document that are most relevant to a specific audience. The very same source document may be presented differently depending on whether the intended reader is a librarian, an administrator, or a technical specialist.

## 12.11 The Structured Abstract

A structured abstract organises its content under clear headings, such as Background, Objective, Method, Results, and Conclusion. This format improves scanning and makes it easy for a reader to locate a specific piece of information quickly.

## 12.12 Editing an Abstract

Editing involves removing repetition, correcting inaccuracies, simplifying unclear wording, checking that the important findings are actually present, and making sure that no personal judgement or invented information has crept into the text.

## 12.13 A Complete Worked Example

Source material: a study examined electronic database use by postgraduate students at three Nigerian universities. Questionnaires were administered to 300 students, producing 240 valid responses. Seventy-eight percent of respondents used databases at least monthly, while 45 percent had difficulty developing effective search strategies. Google Scholar and institutional databases were the most frequently used resources, and the researchers recommended information-literacy training.

## RESULTING INFORMATIVE ABSTRACT

This study examined electronic database use among postgraduate students at three Nigerian universities. Questionnaires were administered to 300 students, producing 240 valid responses. The findings showed that 78% used electronic databases at least monthly, while 45% experienced difficulty developing effective search strategies. Google Scholar and institutional databases were frequently used. The study recommends regular information-literacy training to strengthen students’ database-searching skills.
