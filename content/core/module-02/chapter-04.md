## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:
- Define controlled vocabulary and thesaurus.
- Explain why vocabulary control is necessary.
- Select suitable candidate terms.
- Establish BT, NT, and RT relationships between terms.

## 4.1 What Is a Controlled Vocabulary?

A controlled vocabulary is an organised set of approved terms that are used consistently for indexing and retrieval. By limiting the number of acceptable ways to express a concept, a controlled vocabulary reduces unnecessary variation and improves the match between the words an indexer uses and the words a searcher later types. Well-known examples used in professional practice include the Library of Congress Subject Headings (LCSH), the Sears List of Subject Headings, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), AGROVOC, the ERIC Thesaurus, and the UNESCO Thesaurus.

## 4.2 What Is a Thesaurus?

In information retrieval, a thesaurus is a controlled vocabulary in which the terms are connected through clearly defined relationships, such as BT, NT, and RT. This makes it more than an ordinary dictionary: a dictionary simply defines words, while a retrieval thesaurus is deliberately structured to support indexing and searching.

## 4.3 Selecting Candidate Terms

Candidate terms — the raw material from which a thesaurus is eventually built — may be gathered from document titles, abstracts, author-supplied keywords, full text, existing published vocabularies, reference works, and the everyday language used by the community of people who will actually be searching. Each candidate term is then reviewed for clarity, consistency, how current it is, and how useful it is likely to be for retrieval.

## 4.4 Preferred and Non-Preferred Terms

The preferred term is the specific form of a concept that has been selected for indexing. A non-preferred term is a synonym or near-synonym that directs both users and indexers toward the preferred form instead.

## EXAMPLE

CARS USE AUTOMOBILES — here, Cars is the non-preferred term and Automobiles is the preferred term.

## 4.5 Building BT, NT, and RT Relationships

When building these relationships, direction matters. A narrower concept always points upward to its broader concept using BT, and a broader concept points downward to each of its narrower concepts using NT.

## WORKED EXAMPLE

It would be incorrect to say LIBRARIES BT ACADEMIC LIBRARIES. The correct statement is ACADEMIC LIBRARIES BT LIBRARIES (Academic Libraries is narrower, so its Broader Term is Libraries), and correspondingly LIBRARIES NT ACADEMIC LIBRARIES.

## 4.6 A Complete Thesaurus Entry

A full thesaurus entry typically brings all of these relationships together in one place. For example:

## EXAMPLE ENTRY

UNIVERSITY LIBRARIES — BT: Libraries. NT: Law Libraries, Medical Libraries. RT: Academic Librarians, Digital Libraries. UF: Higher Education Libraries.

## 4.7 The Steps in Thesaurus Construction

Building a thesaurus from scratch is itself a project with a recognisable sequence of stages:
1. Define the subject field the thesaurus will cover.
2. Collect candidate terms from the sources described in section 4.3.
3. Identify synonyms among the candidate terms.
4. Identify homonyms — terms that look alike but carry different meanings.
5. Select the preferred term for each concept.
6. Establish hierarchical (BT/NT) relationships.
7. Establish associative (RT) relationships.
8. Establish equivalence (USE/UF) relationships.
9. Arrange the terms systematically, alphabetically and/or by classified display.
10. Test the thesaurus against real indexing and searching tasks.
11. Revise and update it regularly as terminology changes.
