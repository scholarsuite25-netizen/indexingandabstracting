## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:
- Define an index and identify its main components.
- Distinguish an index from a table of contents.
- List and describe the major types of book indexes.
- Explain the stages involved in preparing a book index.

## 2.1 What Is an Index?

An index is a systematically arranged guide to the contents of a document, usually placed at the end of a book, journal, report, or other publication. It consists of important words, names, subjects, concepts, places, and other significant terms, arranged alphabetically, together with references — usually page numbers — that direct readers to where the information can be found.

An index serves as a pointer or roadmap to information. Instead of reading an entire publication from beginning to end, a reader can consult the index to quickly locate specific information. Knight (1979) defined an index simply as an alphabetical list of names, subjects, or other entries, with references indicating where they occur in a document. Lancaster (2003) went further, describing an index as a device that provides access to information by identifying the concepts discussed in a document and directing users to their locations. Mulvany (2005) emphasised the intellectual side of the work, defining indexing as the process of analysing the content of a document, selecting the significant concepts, and arranging them systematically to facilitate retrieval. Taken together, these definitions make clear that an index is more than a list of page numbers — it is a retrieval tool built on careful intellectual analysis.

## 2.2 Components of an Index

A typical index consists of four elements:
- Headings (main entries) — the primary terms or concepts under which information is listed.
- Subheadings (subentries) — more specific aspects of the main heading, used to organise related information.
- Locators — page numbers, section numbers, or paragraph numbers indicating where the information appears.
- Cross-references — See and See also references that direct readers to related or preferred terms.

## EXAMPLE

Library — automation, 45–52; budgeting, 120–125; collections, 33–41; digitization, 180–195; management, 70–85; services, 95–110. Here “Library” is the main heading, and “automation,” “budgeting,” and the rest are subheadings, each with its own locator.

A good index uses See and See also with a clear division of labour: See directs the reader from a term that is not used to the preferred term (for example, E-books. See Electronic books), while See also directs the reader to a related but distinct topic that may also be worth consulting (for example, Libraries. See also Digital libraries).

## 2.4 Types of Book Indexes

Indexes are not all built to the same purpose. The major types found in books and other publications include:
- Subject index — the most common type, listing topics and concepts.
- Author index — arranged by the names of authors cited or discussed.
- Name index — covering persons, whether authors or not.
- Title index — arranged by the titles of works mentioned.
- Geographical index — arranged by place names.
- Chronological index — arranged by date or period.
- Citation index — showing which works have cited a given publication.
- Analytical index — covering ideas and concepts in depth, often with detailed subentries.
- Keyword index — built from significant words occurring in the text.
- Combined index — merging two or more of the above into a single alphabetical sequence.

## 2.5 Characteristics of a Good Index

An effective index is arranged alphabetically, is accurate and complete, uses clear and familiar terminology, includes important concepts rather than every word that appears, contains precise locators, provides cross-references to related topics, faithfully reflects the actual content of the document, and enables quick, easy retrieval of information.

## 2.6 Book Indexing as an Intellectual Process

Book indexing is the systematic and intellectual process of analysing the content of a book, identifying its significant concepts, subjects, names, places, events, and ideas, and arranging them in alphabetical order with accurate locators so that readers can identify, locate, and retrieve information accurately and efficiently. It is not a mechanical task of extracting words from a text; rather, it requires the indexer to understand the subject matter, determine which concepts matter to readers, select appropriate terms, and organise those terms consistently. Book indexing is particularly valued in academic, scholarly, professional, technical, legal, medical, and reference publications, where readers often seek specific pieces of information rather than reading the entire work sequentially.

## 2.7 The Process of Book Indexing

Preparing a book index generally follows a sequence of stages:
1. Reading the book — the indexer studies the content carefully.
2. Subject analysis — important concepts are identified.
3. Selection of entries — relevant indexing terms are chosen.
4. Preparing main entries — major concepts become headings.
5. Preparing subentries — related ideas are grouped under main headings.
6. Assigning locators — correct page or section numbers are inserted.
7. Creating cross-references — related concepts are linked with See and See also.
8. Alphabetical arrangement — entries are sorted alphabetically.
9. Editing — errors and inconsistencies are corrected.
10. Proofreading — the completed index is verified before publication.

## 2.8 Challenges of Book Indexing

Book indexing is time-consuming, requires genuine subject expertise, and demands consistency across what may be hundreds of entries. Indexers must select appropriate terminology, deal with interdisciplinary topics that do not fit neatly under one heading, and manage synonyms and homonyms. Professional indexing can also be costly, page numbering can shift after late layout changes, and publishing deadlines are often tight — pressures that have only intensified with the rapid growth of digital publications, discussed further in Chapter 14.
