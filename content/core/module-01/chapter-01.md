## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:
- Define subject indexing and subject analysis.
- Explain why indexing is necessary for information retrieval.
- Identify the main objectives and guiding principles of subject indexing.
- Relate indexing decisions to the information needs of users.

## 1.1 What Is Subject Indexing?

Subject indexing is the process of representing what a document is about by assigning terms, phrases, symbols, or other representations that a person can later use to find that document. In simple terms, subject indexing answers two connected questions: what is this document about, and which words or terms should a user search for to find it?

Think of a library, a database, or even a search engine as a very large room full of documents. Without a system that labels each document by its subject matter, a user would have to read every single item to know what it contains. Subject indexing is the labelling system that makes this unnecessary.

Several scholars have refined this basic idea. Lancaster (2003) defines subject indexing as the process of determining the subject content of a document and assigning terms that adequately represent that content for retrieval purposes. Rowley and Farrow (2000) describe it as the assignment of descriptive terms that represent the topics discussed in documents so that they can be identified during information searches. The British Standard BS ISO 5963 frames indexing as the process of examining documents, identifying their subject matter, and selecting indexing terms that accurately represent their contents, while ISO 999 similarly treats indexing as the systematic analysis and representation of the intellectual content of documents to facilitate retrieval. Taken together, these definitions point to three recurring components of subject indexing: subject analysis, selection of indexing terms, and information retrieval.

## 1.2 What Is Subject Analysis?

Subject analysis is the thinking stage that happens before any term is chosen. The indexer reads and examines the document to identify its main topic, the important concepts it discusses, how those concepts relate to one another, and which aspects of the document will matter most to the people who will eventually search for it. An indexer performing subject analysis does not simply copy every word that appears in the title; instead, the indexer interprets the content and decides what truly represents it.

## EXAMPLE

A paper titled “Use of Artificial Intelligence in Nigerian University Libraries” is not just about the words in its title. Careful subject analysis might identify the core concepts as Artificial Intelligence, University Libraries, Nigeria, Library Services, and Information Technology. Which of these are chosen as index terms depends on the indexing language and policy in use.

## 1.3 Why Indexing Matters

Large collections of documents contain far more material than any user could inspect item by item.

Indexing creates organised access points that connect specific documents to the information needs of specific users, making systematic and efficient retrieval possible. Without indexing, search would depend entirely on chance or on browsing everything.

## 1.4 A Short History

Early systems of retrieval relied mainly on access by author and by title. As collections grew larger, it became clear that subject access was also required, since users often know what a document is about before they know who wrote it. Over time, several traditions developed to provide this subject access, including formal subject headings, classification-based indexing, chain indexing, coordinate indexing systems, and, more recently, computer-assisted and machine-supported methods.

## 1.5 How Users Actually Search

Users rarely search using the exact words that an author or an indexer might choose. They may use synonyms, broader or narrower terms, informal phrases, or natural, everyday language that differs from the technical vocabulary of the field. Good indexing practice, supported by controlled vocabularies, helps bridge the gap between how users express their needs and how documents are actually labelled.

## 1.6 The Main Objectives of Subject Indexing

The major objectives of subject indexing can be summarised as follows:
- Accurate subject representation — making sure the index terms genuinely reflect what the document is about.
- Consistent access points — using the same term for the same concept every time, so that related documents are grouped together.
- Efficient retrieval — helping users find what they need with the least possible effort.
- Reduction of ambiguity — avoiding confusion between terms that sound alike but mean different things, or between different terms that mean the same thing.
- Connection of related concepts — showing how ideas relate to one another so that users are not limited to a single narrow search.
- Support for user discovery — helping users find documents they did not already know existed.
- Improved precision and recall — helping ensure that searches return documents that are genuinely relevant, without missing too many that are.

## 1.7 Guiding Principles of Subject Indexing

Beyond its objectives, professional practice is guided by a set of working principles that an indexer applies to every document:
- Specificity — indexing terms should describe the subject as precisely as the vocabulary allows.
- Consistency — the same concept should always be represented using the same authorised term.
- User orientation — terms should reflect the language and search behaviour of the people who will actually be searching.
- Exhaustivity — all significant subjects discussed in the document should be represented.
- Selectivity — only meaningful and genuinely relevant concepts should be indexed, not every word that happens to appear.
- Objectivity — indexing should reflect the document’s actual content rather than the personal opinions of the indexer.

These principles reappear throughout this guide, particularly in Chapter 10, where exhaustivity and specificity are treated as measurable dimensions of indexing quality.

## 1.8 A Note on Assigned Indexing

When an indexer selects terms from a controlled vocabulary or indexing language rather than copying words directly from the document, the result is known as assigned indexing. This is contrasted with derived indexing, covered in Chapter 9, where terms are taken directly from the document’s own words.

Most professional subject indexing in libraries and databases is assigned indexing, because it allows the indexer to represent a concept even when the author never used the preferred term explicitly.
