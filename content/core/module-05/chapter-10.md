## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:
- Explain why indexing systems need to be evaluated.
- Define relevance, exhaustivity, and specificity.
- List the recognised criteria for evaluating an index.
- Explain the significance of the Cranfield research tradition.

## 10.1 Why Evaluate an Indexing System?

Having a large collection of documents and assigning many index terms does not automatically mean that a retrieval system is actually useful to its users. Evaluation provides real evidence about whether users are able to find relevant information efficiently, rather than relying on assumptions.

## 10.2 Relevance

A document is considered relevant when it genuinely satisfies a user’s information need. It is important to understand that relevance is not the same thing as simple keyword matching — a document can contain the exact word a user searched for and still fail to actually answer that user’s question.

## 10.3 Exhaustivity

Exhaustivity refers to the extent to which the important concepts within a document are represented by the index terms assigned to it. A more exhaustive indexing approach provides more conceptual access points into the same document.

## 10.4 Specificity

Specificity refers to how precisely an indexing term represents the actual subject of the document. For a document specifically about university libraries, the term “Libraries” is less specific than the term “University Libraries.”

## 10.5 The Trade-Off Between Exhaustivity and Specificity

Exhaustivity asks how many important concepts were represented, while specificity asks how precisely each individual concept was represented. Indexing that is too broad can increase the retrieval of irrelevant material, even though it may appear to be thorough.

## 10.6 Criteria for Evaluating an Index

Beyond relevance, exhaustivity, and specificity, a comprehensive evaluation of an index examines several further qualities:
- Accuracy — index terms should correctly represent the document’s content.
- Consistency — different indexers should produce reasonably similar results when indexing the same material.
- Usability — users should be able to understand and use the index without special training.
- Currency — the vocabulary should reflect current terminology and changes in the subject field.
- Coverage — the index should adequately cover the documents in the collection as a whole.

## 10.7 The Cranfield Experiments

The Cranfield research programme, closely associated with the researcher Cyril Cleverdon, helped to establish a systematic, evidence-based approach to evaluating retrieval systems. This tradition introduced the use of test collections, sets of queries, and formal relevance judgements, allowing retrieval results to be measured rather than merely asserted. It encouraged experimental comparison between systems, rather than unsupported claims about which indexing approach was best.

## 10.8 Over-Retrieval and Under-Retrieval

Over-retrieval occurs when a search returns too many irrelevant documents. Under-retrieval occurs when relevant documents are missed altogether. The quality of the indexing, the strength of vocabulary control, and the search strategy used can all influence which of these problems occurs.
