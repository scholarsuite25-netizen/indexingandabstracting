## Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:
- Explain automatic and computer-assisted indexing.
- Describe hyperlinked and dynamic indexes in digital publishing.
- Discuss what Artificial Intelligence can and cannot currently do in indexing and abstracting.
- Explain the continuing role of the human indexer and abstractor.

## 14.1 From Manual to Digital Indexing

Traditionally, indexing was carried out entirely by hand: indexers read whole manuscripts, identified important concepts, prepared index cards or handwritten notes, arranged entries alphabetically, and assigned page references. This process was highly labour-intensive, time-consuming, and susceptible to human error. The digital publishing revolution has introduced computer-assisted indexing tools, electronic publishing platforms, digital books, online databases, and Artificial Intelligence, all of which have significantly changed indexing practice. Although the fundamental principles — subject analysis, concept identification, consistency, specificity, and user-centred organisation — remain unchanged, the methods used to apply them have become more sophisticated and efficient.

## 14.2 Automatic Indexing

Automatic indexing uses computer algorithms to identify and assign terms to a document by analysing its titles, abstracts, full text, keywords, metadata, citations, and author information, without an indexer manually assigning every term. Its advantages include rapid processing, the ability to handle large-scale indexing that would overwhelm a purely manual process, reduced manual labour, consistency of application, and suitability for very large collections. Its limitations are equally real: algorithmic context may be misunderstood, ambiguous terms may be incorrectly interpreted, meaningful semantic relationships may be missed altogether, and human quality control is still generally necessary before the results are relied upon.

## 14.3 Computer-Assisted Indexing Software

A distinct category of tool assists, rather than replaces, the professional indexer. Computer-assisted indexing software helps create accurate, consistent, and well-organised indexes while reducing the time and effort required for repetitive tasks, automating routine functions such as sorting entries alphabetically, checking for duplicate entries, maintaining consistent terminology, managing cross-references, and formatting the finished index to a publisher’s specifications. Among the most widely recognised packages are CINDEX, a professional indexing tool widely used by publishers and freelance indexers; Sky Index, a Windows-based application that supports large indexing projects while automatically maintaining alphabetical order and page references; and Macrex, one of the earliest professional indexing programs, particularly popular in academic publishing for its flexibility with large scholarly works and multiple levels of subentries. These tools increase productivity by automating formatting and organisational tasks, freeing the indexer to concentrate on the intellectual work of deciding what deserves an entry.

## 14.4 Hyperlinked and Dynamic Indexes

Electronic books, online textbooks, digital encyclopaedias, institutional repositories, and web-based publications are no longer restricted to static page numbers. Instead, they often use hyperlinks, bookmarks, anchors, and search functions in place of conventional locators. In a hyperlinked index, an entry links directly to the relevant section of the document, so a reader can click through to the required information immediately rather than manually turning to an indicated page. Some digital publications go further still, using dynamic indexes that update automatically whenever the underlying document is revised — unlike a printed index, which must be completely reworked after any change to pagination. Digital indexes may also link to figures, tables, appendices, multimedia resources such as audio and video, external websites, related chapters, and supplementary online materials, so that a modern digital index has evolved from a simple alphabetical list into an interactive navigation system.

## 14.5 What Artificial Intelligence Can Do

AI-powered systems apply Natural Language Processing (NLP), Machine Learning (ML), semantic analysis, and text mining to analyse documents and identify important concepts. In indexing and abstracting work more broadly, AI can assist with:
- extracting keywords automatically;
- identifying frequently occurring concepts;
- recognising the names of people, organisations, and places (named-entity recognition);
- detecting relationships among concepts and performing semantic indexing;
- recommending indexing terms and generating preliminary index entries;
- identifying synonyms and related expressions;
- classifying topics according to subject categories;
- suggesting appropriate cross-references;
- generating metadata and assisting with automatic abstracting; and
- supporting similarity detection, recommendation, and question-answering systems built on top of indexed collections.

These capabilities substantially reduce the time required for preliminary indexing and abstracting, particularly when dealing with lengthy academic publications or very large digital collections.

## 14.6 The Continuing Role of the Human Indexer

Despite these advances, Artificial Intelligence has not replaced the professional indexer or abstractor. Indexing remains fundamentally an intellectual activity that requires human judgement, contextual understanding, subject expertise, and sensitivity to users’ information needs. AI systems can readily identify frequently occurring words, but they often struggle to determine which concepts are truly significant, meanings that are implied rather than stated, the difference between important and trivial information, the contextual meaning of an ambiguous term, the appropriate level of specificity, likely user search behaviour, and conceptual relationships that call for professional interpretation.

## EXAMPLE

An AI system may notice the word “cloud” hundreds of times in a computer science textbook. A professional indexer recognises that the relevant concept is Cloud Computing, not simply “cloud,” and may divide the entry into subentries such as cloud infrastructure, cloud security, and cloud storage. Likewise, a history book may repeatedly mention “Washington” — a human indexer can tell whether this means George Washington, Washington, D.C., or Washington State, ensuring readers are directed accurately.

## 14.7 Benefits and Challenges of Digital and AI-Assisted Indexing

The adoption of digital technologies has brought real benefits: faster index preparation, improved consistency of entries, automatic alphabetical arrangement, efficient management of cross-references, easier correction and revision, better collaboration between publishers and indexers across locations, support for very large indexing projects, interactive hyperlinks in electronic publications, reduced production costs, and improved accessibility through searchable, navigable indexes.

These gains come with real challenges. AI-generated indexes may include irrelevant or excessive entries, because algorithms often rely on word frequency rather than genuine conceptual importance; automated systems may overlook implicit ideas, fail to recognise specialised terminology, or misinterpret context. Other recurring challenges include over-reliance on automated tools, inconsistencies in AI-generated terminology, software compatibility issues across publishing platforms, the need for continual software updates and staff training, difficulty indexing multimedia and interactive content, and ethical concerns relating to algorithmic bias and transparency. These limitations reinforce the importance of professional human oversight throughout the indexing and abstracting process.

## 14.8 Future Trends

The future of indexing and abstracting is expected to involve deeper integration of Artificial Intelligence, semantic technologies, and linked data: AI systems that understand context more accurately, semantic indexing that works with concepts rather than isolated keywords, multilingual indexing for global audiences, voice-search-compatible indexes, and personalised digital indexes that adapt to individual users’ interests and search histories. Cloud computing and collaborative online platforms are also enabling indexers, editors, and publishers to work simultaneously on the same projects regardless of location. As digital libraries and open-access repositories continue to expand, indexing and abstracting will increasingly support the discovery and retrieval of information across vast collections of interconnected resources — with the fundamental principles set out in this guide continuing to underpin that work, whichever tools are used to apply them.
