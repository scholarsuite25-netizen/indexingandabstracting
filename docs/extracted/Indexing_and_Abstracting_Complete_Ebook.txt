MODULE 1 — THEORETICAL FOUNDATIONS AND TYPES OF INDEXES 
Chapter 1: Fundamentals and Objectives of Subject Indexing 
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: 
• Define subject indexing and subject analysis. 
• Explain why indexing is necessary for information retrieval. 
• Identify the main objectives and guiding principles of subject indexing. 
• Relate indexing decisions to the information needs of users. 
 
1.1 What Is Subject Indexing? 
Subject indexing is the process of representing what a document is about by assigning terms, phrases, 
symbols, or other representations that a person can later use to find that document. In simple terms, 
subject indexing answers two connected questions: what is this document about, and which words or 
terms should a user search for to find it? 
Think of a library, a database, or even a search engine as a very large room full of documents. Without a 
system that labels each document by its subject matter, a user would have to read every single item to 
know what it contains. Subject indexing is the labelling system that makes this unnecessary. 
Several scholars have refined this basic idea. Lancaster (2003) defines subject indexing as the process of 
determining the subject content of a document and assigning terms that adequately represent that 
content for retrieval purposes. Rowley and Farrow (2000) describe it as the assignment of descriptive 
terms that represent the topics discussed in documents so that they can be identified during information 
searches. The British Standard BS ISO 5963 frames indexing as the process of examining documents, 
identifying their subject matter, and selecting indexing terms that accurately represent their contents, 
while ISO 999 similarly treats indexing as the systematic analysis and representation of the intellectual 
content of documents to facilitate retrieval. Taken together, these definitions point to three recurring 
components of subject indexing: subject analysis, selection of indexing terms, and information retrieval. 
1.2 What Is Subject Analysis? 
Subject analysis is the thinking stage that happens before any term is chosen. The indexer reads and 
examines the document to identify its main topic, the important concepts it discusses, how those 
concepts relate to one another, and which aspects of the document will matter most to the people who 
will eventually search for it. An indexer performing subject analysis does not simply copy every word that 
appears in the title; instead, the indexer interprets the content and decides what truly represents it. 
EXAMPLE 
A paper titled “Use of Artificial Intelligence in Nigerian University Libraries” is not just about the words 
in its title. Careful subject analysis might identify the core concepts as Artificial Intelligence, University 
Libraries, Nigeria, Library Services, and Information Technology. Which of these are chosen as index 
terms depends on the indexing language and policy in use. 
 
1.3 Why Indexing Matters 
Large collections of documents contain far more material than any user could inspect item by item. 
Indexing creates organised access points that connect specific documents to the information needs of

specific users, making systematic and efficient retrieval possible. Without indexing, search would depend 
entirely on chance or on browsing everything. 
1.4 A Short History 
Early systems of retrieval relied mainly on access by author and by title. As collections grew larger, it 
became clear that subject access was also required, since users often know what a document is about 
before they know who wrote it. Over time, several traditions developed to provide this subject access, 
including formal subject headings, classification-based indexing, chain indexing, coordinate indexing 
systems, and, more recently, computer-assisted and machine-supported methods. 
1.5 How Users Actually Search 
Users rarely search using the exact words that an author or an indexer might choose. They may use 
synonyms, broader or narrower terms, informal phrases, or natural, everyday language that differs from 
the technical vocabulary of the field. Good indexing practice, supported by controlled vocabularies, helps 
bridge the gap between how users express their needs and how documents are actually labelled. 
1.6 The Main Objectives of Subject Indexing 
The major objectives of subject indexing can be summarised as follows: 
• Accurate subject representation — making sure the index terms genuinely reflect what the 
document is about. 
• Consistent access points — using the same term for the same concept every time, so that related 
documents are grouped together. 
• Efficient retrieval — helping users find what they need with the least possible effort. 
• Reduction of ambiguity — avoiding confusion between terms that sound alike but mean different 
things, or between different terms that mean the same thing. 
• Connection of related concepts — showing how ideas relate to one another so that users are not 
limited to a single narrow search. 
• Support for user discovery — helping users find documents they did not already know existed. 
• Improved precision and recall — helping ensure that searches return documents that are genuinely 
relevant, without missing too many that are. 
1.7 Guiding Principles of Subject Indexing 
Beyond its objectives, professional practice is guided by a set of working principles that an indexer applies 
to every document: 
• Specificity — indexing terms should describe the subject as precisely as the vocabulary allows. 
• Consistency — the same concept should always be represented using the same authorised term. 
• User orientation — terms should reflect the language and search behaviour of the people who will 
actually be searching. 
• Exhaustivity — all significant subjects discussed in the document should be represented. 
• Selectivity — only meaningful and genuinely relevant concepts should be indexed, not every word 
that happens to appear. 
• Objectivity — indexing should reflect the document’s actual content rather than the personal 
opinions of the indexer. 
These principles reappear throughout this guide, particularly in Chapter 10, where exhaustivity and 
specificity are treated as measurable dimensions of indexing quality.

1.8 A Note on Assigned Indexing 
When an indexer selects terms from a controlled vocabulary or indexing language rather than copying 
words directly from the document, the result is known as assigned indexing. This is contrasted with 
derived indexing, covered in Chapter 9, where terms are taken directly from the document’s own words. 
Most professional subject indexing in libraries and databases is assigned indexing, because it allows the 
indexer to represent a concept even when the author never used the preferred term explicitly.

Chapter 2: Types of Indexes and Book Indexing 
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: 
• Define an index and identify its main components. 
• Distinguish an index from a table of contents. 
• List and describe the major types of book indexes. 
• Explain the stages involved in preparing a book index. 
 
2.1 What Is an Index? 
An index is a systematically arranged guide to the contents of a document, usually placed at the end of a 
book, journal, report, or other publication. It consists of important words, names, subjects, concepts, 
places, and other significant terms, arranged alphabetically, together with references — usually page 
numbers — that direct readers to where the information can be found. 
An index serves as a pointer or roadmap to information. Instead of reading an entire publication from 
beginning to end, a reader can consult the index to quickly locate specific information. Knight (1979) 
defined an index simply as an alphabetical list of names, subjects, or other entries, with references 
indicating where they occur in a document. Lancaster (2003) went further, describing an index as a device 
that provides access to information by identifying the concepts discussed in a document and directing 
users to their locations. Mulvany (2005) emphasised the intellectual side of the work, defining indexing as 
the process of analysing the content of a document, selecting the significant concepts, and arranging 
them systematically to facilitate retrieval. Taken together, these definitions make clear that an index is 
more than a list of page numbers — it is a retrieval tool built on careful intellectual analysis. 
2.2 Components of an Index 
A typical index consists of four elements: 
• Headings (main entries) — the primary terms or concepts under which information is listed. 
• Subheadings (subentries) — more specific aspects of the main heading, used to organise related 
information.

• Locators — page numbers, section numbers, or paragraph numbers indicating where the 
information appears. 
• Cross-references — See and See also references that direct readers to related or preferred terms. 
EXAMPLE 
Library — automation, 45–52; budgeting, 120–125; collections, 33–41; digitization, 180–195; 
management, 70–85; services, 95–110. Here “Library” is the main heading, and “automation,” 
“budgeting,” and the rest are subheadings, each with its own locator. 
 
A good index uses See and See also with a clear division of labour: See directs the reader from a term that 
is not used to the preferred term (for example, E-books. See Electronic books), while See also directs the 
reader to a related but distinct topic that may also be worth consulting (for example, Libraries. See also 
Digital libraries).

2.4 Types of Book Indexes 
Indexes are not all built to the same purpose. The major types found in books and other publications 
include: 
• Subject index — the most common type, listing topics and concepts. 
• Author index — arranged by the names of authors cited or discussed. 
• Name index — covering persons, whether authors or not. 
• Title index — arranged by the titles of works mentioned. 
• Geographical index — arranged by place names. 
• Chronological index — arranged by date or period. 
• Citation index — showing which works have cited a given publication. 
• Analytical index — covering ideas and concepts in depth, often with detailed subentries. 
• Keyword index — built from significant words occurring in the text. 
• Combined index — merging two or more of the above into a single alphabetical sequence. 
2.5 Characteristics of a Good Index

An effective index is arranged alphabetically, is accurate and complete, uses clear and familiar 
terminology, includes important concepts rather than every word that appears, contains precise locators, 
provides cross-references to related topics, faithfully reflects the actual content of the document, and 
enables quick, easy retrieval of information. 
2.6 Book Indexing as an Intellectual Process 
Book indexing is the systematic and intellectual process of analysing the content of a book, identifying its 
significant concepts, subjects, names, places, events, and ideas, and arranging them in alphabetical order 
with accurate locators so that readers can identify, locate, and retrieve information accurately and 
efficiently. It is not a mechanical task of extracting words from a text; rather, it requires the indexer to 
understand the subject matter, determine which concepts matter to readers, select appropriate terms, 
and organise those terms consistently. Book indexing is particularly valued in academic, scholarly, 
professional, technical, legal, medical, and reference publications, where readers often seek specific 
pieces of information rather than reading the entire work sequentially. 
2.7 The Process of Book Indexing 
Preparing a book index generally follows a sequence of stages: 
1. Reading the book — the indexer studies the content carefully. 
2. Subject analysis — important concepts are identified. 
3. Selection of entries — relevant indexing terms are chosen. 
4. Preparing main entries — major concepts become headings. 
5. Preparing subentries — related ideas are grouped under main headings. 
6. Assigning locators — correct page or section numbers are inserted. 
7. Creating cross-references — related concepts are linked with See and See also. 
8. Alphabetical arrangement — entries are sorted alphabetically. 
9. Editing — errors and inconsistencies are corrected. 
10. Proofreading — the completed index is verified before publication. 
2.8 Challenges of Book Indexing 
Book indexing is time-consuming, requires genuine subject expertise, and demands consistency across 
what may be hundreds of entries. Indexers must select appropriate terminology, deal with 
interdisciplinary topics that do not fit neatly under one heading, and manage synonyms and homonyms. 
Professional indexing can also be costly, page numbering can shift after late layout changes, and 
publishing deadlines are often tight — pressures that have only intensified with the rapid growth of digital 
publications, discussed further in Chapter 14.

• Explain the meaning of semantics and syntax as they apply to indexing. 
• Identify equivalence, hierarchical, and associative relationships between terms. 
• Explain syntactic control, role operators, and citation order. 
 
3.1 Semantics: The Study of Meaning 
In everyday language, semantics is simply the study of meaning. In indexing, semantic relationships 
describe how concepts and the terms that represent them relate to one another. Three relationships are 
especially important in indexing work: equivalence, hierarchy, and association. 
3.2 Equivalence 
Equivalence connects two or more different terms that can represent the same concept, or concepts that 
are close enough to be treated as the same for retrieval purposes. In a controlled vocabulary, one of these 
terms is usually chosen as the preferred term, while the others are marked as non-preferred and simply 
point the user toward the preferred form. 
3.3 Hierarchy 
A hierarchical relationship connects a general concept to a more specific one. A Broader Term (often 
abbreviated BT) represents the more general concept, while a Narrower Term (NT) represents the more 
specific one. 
EXAMPLE 
Libraries is a Broader Term for University Libraries; University Libraries is a Narrower Term for 
Libraries. 
 
3.4 Association 
A Related Term (RT) connects two concepts that are meaningfully associated with one another but are 
neither synonyms nor in a broader–narrower relationship. For instance, the concepts Libraries and 
Librarians are closely related in meaning, even though neither is a broader or narrower version of the 
other. 
3.5 Syntax: The Study of Order and Combination 
While semantics is about meaning, syntax is about how concepts are combined or ordered when they are 
expressed together. Good syntactic control prevents an index entry from becoming a meaningless list of 
separate words, and instead preserves the relationships that exist among the concepts being described. 
3.6 Role Operators 
Role operators identify the function that each concept plays within a subject statement — for example, 
distinguishing an action from the person or thing performing it, or from the object being acted upon. Role 
operators are especially important in structured indexing systems such as PRECIS. Because different 
courses and institutions may use slightly different notations, students should always learn and apply the 
exact operator table prescribed in their own course manual. 
3.7 Citation Order

Citation order refers to the sequence in which concepts appear within a coordinated indexing expression. 
Changing that order can change the emphasis of the statement, and in some cases can change its 
meaning altogether, which is why citation order is treated as a deliberate design choice rather than an 
arbitrary one.

MODULE 2 — VOCABULARY CONTROL 
Chapter 4: Thesaurus Construction: Design and Term Selection 
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: 
• Define controlled vocabulary and thesaurus. 
• Explain why vocabulary control is necessary. 
• Select suitable candidate terms. 
• Establish BT, NT, and RT relationships between terms. 
 
4.1 What Is a Controlled Vocabulary? 
A controlled vocabulary is an organised set of approved terms that are used consistently for indexing and 
retrieval. By limiting the number of acceptable ways to express a concept, a controlled vocabulary reduces 
unnecessary variation and improves the match between the words an indexer uses and the words a 
searcher later types. Well-known examples used in professional practice include the Library of Congress 
Subject Headings (LCSH), the Sears List of Subject Headings, Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), AGROVOC, 
the ERIC Thesaurus, and the UNESCO Thesaurus. 
4.2 What Is a Thesaurus? 
In information retrieval, a thesaurus is a controlled vocabulary in which the terms are connected through 
clearly defined relationships, such as BT, NT, and RT. This makes it more than an ordinary dictionary: a 
dictionary simply defines words, while a retrieval thesaurus is deliberately structured to support indexing 
and searching. 
4.3 Selecting Candidate Terms 
Candidate terms — the raw material from which a thesaurus is eventually built — may be gathered from 
document titles, abstracts, author-supplied keywords, full text, existing published vocabularies, reference 
works, and the everyday language used by the community of people who will actually be searching. Each 
candidate term is then reviewed for clarity, consistency, how current it is, and how useful it is likely to be 
for retrieval. 
4.4 Preferred and Non-Preferred Terms 
The preferred term is the specific form of a concept that has been selected for indexing. A non-preferred 
term is a synonym or near-synonym that directs both users and indexers toward the preferred form 
instead. 
EXAMPLE 
CARS USE AUTOMOBILES — here, Cars is the non-preferred term and Automobiles is the preferred 
term. 
 
4.5 Building BT, NT, and RT Relationships 
When building these relationships, direction matters. A narrower concept always points upward to its 
broader concept using BT, and a broader concept points downward to each of its narrower concepts using 
NT.

WORKED EXAMPLE 
It would be incorrect to say LIBRARIES BT ACADEMIC LIBRARIES. The correct statement is ACADEMIC

4.6 A Complete Thesaurus Entry 
A full thesaurus entry typically brings all of these relationships together in one place. For example: 
EXAMPLE ENTRY 
UNIVERSITY LIBRARIES — BT: Libraries. NT: Law Libraries, Medical Libraries. RT: Academic Librarians, 
Digital Libraries. UF: Higher Education Libraries. 
 
4.7 The Steps in Thesaurus Construction 
Building a thesaurus from scratch is itself a project with a recognisable sequence of stages: 
1. Define the subject field the thesaurus will cover. 
2. Collect candidate terms from the sources described in section 4.3. 
3. Identify synonyms among the candidate terms. 
4. Identify homonyms — terms that look alike but carry different meanings. 
5. Select the preferred term for each concept. 
6. Establish hierarchical (BT/NT) relationships. 
7. Establish associative (RT) relationships. 
8. Establish equivalence (USE/UF) relationships. 
9. Arrange the terms systematically, alphabetically and/or by classified display. 
10. Test the thesaurus against real indexing and searching tasks. 
11. Revise and update it regularly as terminology changes.

Chapter 5: Thesaurus Construction: Structure and Maintenance 
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

• Use USE and UF references correctly. 
• Distinguish between alphabetical and classified thesaurus displays. 
• Describe the basic tasks involved in thesaurus maintenance. 
 
5.1 Scope Notes

A scope note is a short explanatory statement attached to a term to clarify exactly how that term is to be 
interpreted or applied within a particular vocabulary. Scope notes are especially useful when a term is 
ambiguous in everyday language or when it carries a specialised meaning within the subject field. 
5.2 USE and UF References 
USE directs a searcher or indexer from a non-preferred term toward the preferred term that should be 
used instead. UF, which stands for Used For, works in the opposite direction: it is displayed underneath 
the preferred term to show which non-preferred terms lead to it. 
EXAMPLE 
AUTOMOBILES — UF: Cars. | CARS — USE: Automobiles. 
 
5.3 Alphabetical Display 
In an alphabetical display, terms are simply arranged from A to Z. This arrangement is convenient for a 
user who already knows the specific term they are interested in and wants to look up its relationships 
quickly. 
5.4 Classified Display 
In a classified display, terms are grouped together within a broader conceptual structure rather than 
alphabetically. This arrangement supports browsing, allowing a user to explore related areas of a subject 
even when they do not yet know the exact term they need. 
5.5 Maintaining a Thesaurus 
A thesaurus is never a finished product. Because terminology changes over time, thesauri require 
continuing revision. Typical maintenance tasks include adding new terms as they emerge, revising 
definitions and scope notes, merging or splitting concepts as understanding of a field develops, updating 
cross-references, and keeping a documented record of why each decision was made. 
5.6 The Role of Software 
Thesaurus-management software can support many of the mechanical aspects of this work, including 
data entry, checking that relationships are consistent, version control, and publication of the finished 
thesaurus. However, such software supports the intellectual work of the indexer; it does not replace the 
human judgement required to decide what a term means and how it relates to other terms.

MODULE 3 — PRE-COORDINATE INDEXING SYSTEMS 
Chapter 6: Classic Pre-Coordinate Techniques 
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: 
• Define pre-coordinate indexing. 
• Explain chain indexing. 
• Explain cyclic (cycled) indexing. 
• Discuss the advantages and limitations of pre-coordinate systems. 
 
6.1 What Is Pre-Coordinate Indexing? 
In pre-coordinate indexing, the concepts that make up a subject are combined into a single indexing 
expression at the moment the document is indexed — that is, before any user ever performs a search. 
The indexer, not the searcher, is responsible for deciding how the different concepts relate to one 
another within that expression. 
EXAMPLE 
University Libraries — Automation — Nigeria. The relationship between these three concepts, and the 
order in which they appear, is determined entirely by the indexer at the time of indexing. 
 
6.2 Key Characteristics 
Pre-coordinate systems can produce carefully controlled and well-structured representations of a 
document’s subject, and are common in traditional subject-heading systems. Their main limitation is that 
the indexer must try to anticipate which combinations of concepts will actually be useful to future users, 
which is not always possible to predict with certainty, and skilled, trained indexers are needed to apply 
the rules correctly. 
6.3 Chain Indexing 
Chain indexing is closely associated with the Indian librarian and classification theorist S. R. Ranganathan. 
This technique derives subject entries from the hierarchical chain of concepts found within a classification 
scheme, and it creates access points directly from that conceptual structure, illustrating the close 
relationship between classification and subject indexing. 
6.4 Cyclic (Cycled) Indexing 
Cyclic indexing approaches generate multiple index entries by varying the position or focus of terms 
within a compound subject, so that each significant component is, in turn, brought into the leading 
position. This provides several different access points that all lead back to the same document, increasing 
the chance that a user will find it regardless of which term they search first. 
WORKED EXAMPLE 
The subject “Use of Artificial Intelligence in University Libraries in Nigeria” could generate cycled 
access points such as: Artificial Intelligence — University Libraries — Nigeria; University Libraries — 
Artificial Intelligence — Nigeria; and Nigeria — University Libraries — Artificial Intelligence.

6.5 Advantages and Limitations 
Pre-coordinate systems offer several advantages, alongside some real limitations: 
• Advantage — controlled and predictable representation of subjects. 
• Advantage — explicit relationships between concepts are preserved. 
• Advantage — access points are consistent and predictable for users. 
• Advantage — can provide highly precise subject access. 
• Limitation — considerable intellectual effort is required from the indexer. 
• Limitation — the system depends heavily on the rules being applied correctly. 
• Limitation — the combinations a user actually searches for may differ from those the indexer 
anticipated. 
• Limitation — users may need to understand the indexing structure to search it effectively.

Chapter 7: Permuted and Algorithmic Systems 
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: 
• Explain what SLIC is. 
• Explain the purpose and origin of PRECIS. 
• Understand the role of operators and string generation. 
 
7.1 SLIC (Selective Listing in Combination) 
SLIC stands for Selective Listing in Combination. It is a pre-coordinate approach that provides multiple 
access points by generating selected combinations of the terms assigned to a document, emphasising the 
analysis of subject concepts and their relationships when constructing index entries. 
7.2 PRECIS (Preserved Context Index System) 
PRECIS stands for Preserved Context Index System. It was developed by the British indexer Derek Austin 
for the British National Bibliography, and was designed specifically to preserve the context and the 
relationships between concepts within a subject statement while still generating a set of usable index 
entries automatically, once the necessary input had been supplied. Its major contribution was its attempt 
to preserve the meaning and context of concepts, rather than merely listing isolated keywords, and it was 
significant in the wider development of computer-assisted subject indexing. 
7.3 Role Operators in PRECIS 
Role operators indicate the function that each concept plays within a subject statement — for example, 
distinguishing an action from its agent, its object, or another role it might play. Because the exact symbols 
and rules used can differ between manuals, students should always learn and apply the version 
prescribed by their own lecturer. 
7.4 How a PRECIS String Is Built

Building a PRECIS-style index string generally follows a practical sequence: 
1. Understand the subject of the document as a whole. 
2. Identify the individual concepts contained within that subject. 
3. Identify the role that each concept plays. 
4. Apply the prescribed role operators to each concept. 
5. Generate the finished index string from the ordered, labelled concepts. 
7.5 Why Preserving Context Matters 
Consider a simple, unordered list of terms: students; libraries; databases; use. This list does not make 
clear who is using what, or what the actual relationship between these concepts is. A syntactic system 
such as PRECIS is designed precisely to prevent this kind of ambiguity by preserving the relationships 
between the concepts.

MODULE 4 — POST-COORDINATE AND DERIVED INDEXING 
Chapter 8: Post-Coordinate Indexing Systems 
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: 
• Define post-coordinate indexing. 
• Explain how terms are combined at the point of retrieval. 
• Describe free-text searching, Uniterm indexing, and optical coincidence systems. 
 
8.1 What Is Post-Coordinate Indexing? 
In post-coordinate indexing, concepts are assigned to a document separately, as individual terms, and it is 
the searcher or the retrieval system that combines them together at the moment of searching, rather 
than the indexer combining them in advance. 
EXAMPLE 
A document might simply receive the three separate terms Artificial Intelligence, University Libraries, 
and Reference Services, without any single combined expression being created in advance. A user 
could later combine any two or three of these with AND to retrieve it. 
 
8.2 Pre-Coordinate versus Post-Coordinate 
The essential difference is timing. Pre-coordinate indexing combines concepts while the document is 
being indexed. Post-coordinate indexing leaves each term separately searchable and lets the combination 
happen later, during retrieval. 
8.3 Free-Text Searching 
Free-text searching looks for words directly within fields such as titles, abstracts, or the full text of a 
document, without relying entirely on a controlled vocabulary. This approach is flexible, but it can suffer 
from problems such as synonyms not being matched, ambiguous words, and variations in spelling. 
8.4 Uniterm Indexing 
Uniterm indexing is an early and influential example of the post-coordinate approach. It works by 
assigning individual, single terms to documents and then combining those terms only at the point of 
retrieval, illustrating the basic principle behind all post-coordinate systems. 
8.5 Optical Coincidence Systems 
Historically, optical coincidence systems used physical cards or other machine-readable formats to 
identify which documents shared a chosen set of terms. Modern digital Boolean searching performs the 
same essential logical operations, but does so electronically rather than mechanically. 
8.6 Advantages and Limitations 
Post-coordinate systems allow for flexible and often unexpected combinations of terms at search time, 
are well suited to computerised retrieval, and scale well to large databases. However, this flexibility 
comes at a cost: it places greater responsibility on the searcher to construct an effective query, results can 
be affected by inconsistent or uncontrolled terminology, and a poorly formed search can produce high 
recall but low precision.

Chapter 9: Search Strategies and Derived Indexing 
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: 
• Explain how a search strategy is formulated. 
• Use basic Boolean operators. 
• Explain what derived indexing is, including its advantages and disadvantages.

9.1 What Is a Search Strategy? 
A search strategy is a planned approach to translating a real information need into searchable concepts, 
specific terms, and useful combinations of those terms. Searching is generally an iterative activity, 
meaning that a searcher typically refines their approach over several attempts rather than getting the 
perfect result in a single step. 
9.2 Boolean Operators 
Three basic Boolean operators are used to combine search terms: 
• AND narrows a search by requiring that both concepts be present. 
• OR broadens a search by accepting either concept. 
• NOT excludes a specified concept from the results. 
EXAMPLE 
LIBRARIES AND ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE narrows a search to documents containing both concepts. 
CARS OR AUTOMOBILES broadens a search to accept either term. 
 
9.3 Formulating a Search 
A typical search formulation follows these steps: 
1. Start by identifying the major concepts in the information need. 
2. Identify synonyms and related terms for each concept. 
3. Run an initial search using these terms. 
4. Inspect the records retrieved for useful results and new vocabulary. 
5. Refine the query, using controlled vocabulary terms where they are available. 
9.4 Derived Indexing 
Derived indexing obtains its index terms directly from features of the document itself — most commonly 
the title, but also abstracts, full text, author-supplied keywords, and captions. This approach can be fast 
and economical, since it requires little additional intellectual effort and is well suited to automation, but 
its quality depends entirely on how well the source field actually represents the document’s subject.

Because derived terms are taken from the author’s own words rather than a controlled vocabulary, they 
may include irrelevant words, fail to capture implied context, or run into synonym and ambiguity 
problems that a trained indexer would normally resolve. 
9.5 KWIC (Keyword in Context) 
KWIC stands for Keyword in Context. In a KWIC display, a significant keyword is shown together with the 
words surrounding it in the title, so that the keyword retains the context in which it originally appeared. 
9.6 KWOC (Keyword Out of Context) 
KWOC stands for Keyword Out of Context. In a KWOC display, the keyword is shown separately from the 
rest of the title, typically as a heading, followed underneath by the full citation. 
9.7 Comparing KWIC and KWOC 
KWIC keeps each keyword embedded within its surrounding title, preserving context at a glance. KWOC 
instead places the keyword outside the title as a separate heading. Both techniques create multiple access 
points from terms that have been derived directly from the document, rather than assigned by an 
indexer.

Chapter 10: Evaluation Methodologies 
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: 
• Explain why indexing systems need to be evaluated. 
• Define relevance, exhaustivity, and specificity. 
• List the recognised criteria for evaluating an index. 
• Explain the significance of the Cranfield research tradition. 
 
10.1 Why Evaluate an Indexing System? 
Having a large collection of documents and assigning many index terms does not automatically mean that 
a retrieval system is actually useful to its users. Evaluation provides real evidence about whether users are 
able to find relevant information efficiently, rather than relying on assumptions. 
10.2 Relevance 
A document is considered relevant when it genuinely satisfies a user’s information need. It is important to 
understand that relevance is not the same thing as simple keyword matching — a document can contain 
the exact word a user searched for and still fail to actually answer that user’s question. 
10.3 Exhaustivity 
Exhaustivity refers to the extent to which the important concepts within a document are represented by 
the index terms assigned to it. A more exhaustive indexing approach provides more conceptual access 
points into the same document. 
10.4 Specificity 
Specificity refers to how precisely an indexing term represents the actual subject of the document. For a 
document specifically about university libraries, the term “Libraries” is less specific than the term 
“University Libraries.” 
10.5 The Trade-Off Between Exhaustivity and Specificity 
Exhaustivity asks how many important concepts were represented, while specificity asks how precisely 
each individual concept was represented. Indexing that is too broad can increase the retrieval of 
irrelevant material, even though it may appear to be thorough. 
10.6 Criteria for Evaluating an Index 
Beyond relevance, exhaustivity, and specificity, a comprehensive evaluation of an index examines several 
further qualities: 
• Accuracy — index terms should correctly represent the document’s content. 
• Consistency — different indexers should produce reasonably similar results when indexing the same 
material. 
• Usability — users should be able to understand and use the index without special training. 
• Currency — the vocabulary should reflect current terminology and changes in the subject field. 
• Coverage — the index should adequately cover the documents in the collection as a whole. 
10.7 The Cranfield Experiments 


===== PAGE 21 =====
LIS 815 • INDEXING AND ABSTRACTING

21 
The Cranfield research programme, closely associated with the researcher Cyril Cleverdon, helped to 
establish a systematic, evidence-based approach to evaluating retrieval systems. This tradition introduced 
the use of test collections, sets of queries, and formal relevance judgements, allowing retrieval results to 
be measured rather than merely asserted. It encouraged experimental comparison between systems, 
rather than unsupported claims about which indexing approach was best. 
10.8 Over-Retrieval and Under-Retrieval 
Over-retrieval occurs when a search returns too many irrelevant documents. Under-retrieval occurs when 
relevant documents are missed altogether. The quality of the indexing, the strength of vocabulary control, 
and the search strategy used can all influence which of these problems occurs.

Chapter 11: Precision, Recall, and Search Optimisation 
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: 
• Define and calculate precision, recall, and fallout. 
• Explain the meaning of TP, FP, FN, and TN. 
• Explain the trade-off between precision and recall. 
• Apply basic techniques of search optimisation. 
 
11.1 Four Possible Outcomes 
Every document in a collection, in relation to a given search, falls into one of four possible categories: 
• True Positive (TP): the document is relevant and was retrieved. 
• False Positive (FP): the document is not relevant, but was retrieved anyway. 
• False Negative (FN): the document is relevant, but was not retrieved. 
• True Negative (TN): the document is not relevant and was correctly not retrieved. 
11.2 Precision 
Precision answers the question: of everything that was retrieved, how much of it was actually relevant? 
FORMULA 
Precision = Relevant Retrieved ÷ Total Retrieved = TP ÷ (TP + FP) 
 
11.3 Recall 
Recall answers a different question: of all the relevant documents that exist in the collection, how many 
of them were actually retrieved? 


===== PAGE 22 =====
LIS 815 • INDEXING AND ABSTRACTING 
A Beginner-Friendly Guide to LIS 815 
22 
FORMULA 
Recall = Relevant Retrieved ÷ Total Relevant = TP ÷ (TP + FN) 
 
11.4 A Simple Worked Example 
Suppose a collection contains 100 relevant documents in total. A search retrieves 20 documents, of which 
15 turn out to be relevant. 
CALCULATION 
Precision = 15 ÷ 20 = 75%. Recall = 15 ÷ 100 = 15%. This search produced fairly clean results, but it 
missed a large number of the relevant documents that were actually available. 
 
11.5 Fallout 
Fallout measures the proportion of non-relevant documents that were retrieved, out of all the non-
relevant documents that exist in the collection. 
FORMULA 
Fallout = Non-Relevant Retrieved ÷ Total Non-Relevant = FP ÷ (FP + TN) 
 
11.6 A Fuller Worked Example 
Suppose a collection contains 1,000 documents in total, of which 200 are relevant to a particular search. 
That search retrieves 100 documents, of which 80 are relevant. 
CALCULATION 
Precision = 80 ÷ 100 = 80%. Recall = 80 ÷ 200 = 40%. Non-relevant retrieved = 20; total non-relevant = 
800; Fallout = 20 ÷ 800 = 2.5%. 
 
11.7 The Precision–Recall Trade-Off 
A broad search strategy tends to increase recall, but it often retrieves more irrelevant results along with it, 
which reduces precision. A narrow search strategy tends to improve precision, but risks missing relevant 
documents. The appropriate balance between the two depends entirely on the specific information need 
of the user. 
11.8 Improving Recall 
• Use synonyms and related terms. 
• Broaden the search appropriately. 
• Use the OR operator. 
• Search additional fields, not only the title. 
• Make use of controlled-vocabulary relationships such as BT and NT. 
• Inspect useful results for additional relevant terminology.

Chapter 12: Abstracting Techniques and Types 
By the end of this chapter, you should be able to: 
• Define an abstract. 
• Explain content extraction and editing. 
• Distinguish between indicative, informative, critical, slant, author's, and structured abstracts.

12.1 What Is an Abstract? 
An abstract is a concise representation of a document’s content. Depending on its particular type, an 
abstract may present the purpose of a study, the method used, the main findings, and the conclusion 
reached. 
12.2 Why Abstracts Matter 
Abstracts help users screen large amounts of literature quickly and decide which documents genuinely 
deserve a full reading. They are particularly important within bibliographic and indexing-and-abstracting 
databases, where a user may be scanning hundreds of records at once. 
12.3 Content Extraction 
When preparing an abstract, the writer should ask a consistent set of questions of the source document: 
• What is the problem being addressed? 
• What is the objective of the work? 
• What exactly was studied? 
• How was it studied — what method was used? 
• What were the main findings? 
• What conclusion or recommendation was reached? 
12.4 Principles of Good Abstracting 
A good abstract should be clear, concise, accurate, objective, self-contained, coherent, grammatically 
correct, and faithful to the source document. It should never introduce unsupported claims, invented 
information, or the personal opinions of the person writing the abstract. 
12.5 The Indicative Abstract 
An indicative abstract tells the reader what a document covers and answers the question “What does this 
document discuss?”, but it usually does not report detailed findings. It is generally brief. 
EXAMPLE 
This article discusses the application of artificial intelligence in university libraries, examining major 
uses, potential benefits, implementation challenges, and implications for future services. 
 
12.6 The Informative Abstract

25 
An informative abstract provides substantive information, most commonly including the purpose, 
method, findings, and conclusion of a study. It is detailed enough that a reader can often use it to judge 
whether the original document is worth reading in full. 
EXAMPLE 
This study examined AI adoption among university librarians in Nigeria. A questionnaire was 
administered to librarians in selected universities. The findings showed high awareness but limited 
formal training, along with concerns about accuracy and data privacy. The study recommends regular 
AI-literacy programmes and institutional guidance. 
 
12.7 The Indicative-Informative Abstract 
As the name suggests, an indicative-informative abstract combines features of both types above: it 
indicates the general scope of the document while also reporting some of its substantive findings, without 
going into the full detail of a purely informative abstract. 
12.8 The Critical Abstract 
A critical abstract goes a step further than a purely descriptive summary. In addition to summarising the 
content, it offers an assessment or evaluation of the document — commenting, for example, on the 
strengths, weaknesses, methodology, reliability, or significance of the work. Because it involves 
judgement rather than pure description, a critical abstract is usually prepared by someone with subject 
expertise. 
12.9 The Author's Abstract 
An author’s abstract is simply an abstract prepared by the author of the original document, rather than by 
an independent indexer or abstractor. Many journals require authors to submit one alongside their 
article. 
12.10 The Slant Abstract 
A slant abstract emphasises the particular aspects of a document that are most relevant to a specific 
audience. The very same source document may be presented differently depending on whether the 
intended reader is a librarian, an administrator, or a technical specialist. 
12.11 The Structured Abstract 
A structured abstract organises its content under clear headings, such as Background, Objective, Method, 
Results, and Conclusion. This format improves scanning and makes it easy for a reader to locate a specific 
piece of information quickly. 
12.12 Editing an Abstract 
Editing involves removing repetition, correcting inaccuracies, simplifying unclear wording, checking that 
the important findings are actually present, and making sure that no personal judgement or invented 
information has crept into the text.

13.1 Abstracts in Databases 
Bibliographic and indexing-and-abstracting databases typically contain titles, authors, publication details, 
assigned indexing terms, and abstracts. The abstract helps a user judge the relevance of a document 
before deciding whether to obtain the full text. 
13.2 Current Awareness Services (CAS) 
Current Awareness Services keep users informed about newly available information within a subject area 
of interest to them. Alerts sent through such services may include citations together with abstracts, 
allowing users to quickly identify newly published material that is relevant to their work. 
13.3 Selective Dissemination of Information (SDI) 
Selective Dissemination of Information distributes newly available material according to the individual 
interests or profile of each user. Abstracts help recipients of an SDI service decide quickly whether a newly 
identified document is actually useful to them. 
13.4 Abstracts and Information Retrieval 


===== PAGE 27 =====

27 
A single search can return hundreds of records. Reading the abstracts attached to those records allows a 
user to screen them efficiently, rather than having to read every full document, and to select only the 
most promising ones for closer reading. 
13.5 Major Indexing and Abstracting Services 
Indexing and abstracting services provide organised access to scholarly and professional literature across 
disciplines, combining the indexing and abstracting principles covered throughout this guide into large-
scale, continuously updated products. Well-known examples include Scopus, Web of Science, 
PubMed/MEDLINE, the ERIC database, Chemical Abstracts, Library and Information Science Abstracts 
(LISA), Library Literature & Information Science Full Text, and the EBSCO and ProQuest families of 
databases. These services assist researchers in discovering literature across different disciplines, and are 
themselves a practical demonstration of subject indexing and abstracting at scale. 
13.6 Automated Summarisation 
Automated summarisation uses computational methods to produce shorter representations of 
documents. It becomes especially useful when very large collections of material must be processed 
quickly, at a scale that would be impractical for human abstractors alone. 
13.7 Extractive Summarisation 
Extractive methods work by selecting important words, phrases, or whole sentences directly from the 
source document. The resulting summary is therefore built largely from text that already existed in the 
original. 
13.8 Abstractive Summarisation 
Abstractive methods attempt to represent the meaning of the source document using newly generated 
wording, rather than simply lifting text from the original. Modern AI systems can assist with this kind of 
summarisation, but their output may sometimes omit important details or distort the original meaning, so 
it always requires careful checking against the source. 
13.9 Human Abstractors versus Automated Systems 
Human abstractors bring contextual judgement to their work and can tailor an abstract for a specialised 
audience in a way that automated systems currently cannot match. Automated systems, in turn, offer 
speed and the ability to work at a much larger scale. In practice, a workflow that combines automated 
assistance with human quality control often produces the best results. 
13.10 The Complete Workflow 
The relationship between indexing and abstracting can be summarised as a single continuous workflow: a 
document undergoes subject analysis, is indexed, is abstracted, becomes part of a database record, is 
found through a user’s search, is screened by the user through its record, and is finally selected for full-
text reading. Indexing provides the structured access points that make a document findable in the first 
place; abstracting then provides the concise representation of content that helps the user decide whether 
it is worth reading in full.

14.1 From Manual to Digital Indexing 
Traditionally, indexing was carried out entirely by hand: indexers read whole manuscripts, identified 
important concepts, prepared index cards or handwritten notes, arranged entries alphabetically, and 
assigned page references. This process was highly labour-intensive, time-consuming, and susceptible to 
human error. The digital publishing revolution has introduced computer-assisted indexing tools, electronic 
publishing platforms, digital books, online databases, and Artificial Intelligence, all of which have 
significantly changed indexing practice. Although the fundamental principles — subject analysis, concept 
identification, consistency, specificity, and user-centred organisation — remain unchanged, the methods 
used to apply them have become more sophisticated and efficient. 
14.2 Automatic Indexing 
Automatic indexing uses computer algorithms to identify and assign terms to a document by analysing its 
titles, abstracts, full text, keywords, metadata, citations, and author information, without an indexer 
manually assigning every term. Its advantages include rapid processing, the ability to handle large-scale 
indexing that would overwhelm a purely manual process, reduced manual labour, consistency of 
application, and suitability for very large collections. Its limitations are equally real: algorithmic context 
may be misunderstood, ambiguous terms may be incorrectly interpreted, meaningful semantic 
relationships may be missed altogether, and human quality control is still generally necessary before the 
results are relied upon. 
14.3 Computer-Assisted Indexing Software 
A distinct category of tool assists, rather than replaces, the professional indexer. Computer-assisted 
indexing software helps create accurate, consistent, and well-organised indexes while reducing the time 
and effort required for repetitive tasks, automating routine functions such as sorting entries 
alphabetically, checking for duplicate entries, maintaining consistent terminology, managing cross-
references, and formatting the finished index to a publisher’s specifications. Among the most widely 
recognised packages are CINDEX, a professional indexing tool widely used by publishers and freelance 
indexers; Sky Index, a Windows-based application that supports large indexing projects while 
automatically maintaining alphabetical order and page references; and Macrex, one of the earliest 
professional indexing programs, particularly popular in academic publishing for its flexibility with large 
scholarly works and multiple levels of subentries. These tools increase productivity by automating 
formatting and organisational tasks, freeing the indexer to concentrate on the intellectual work of 
deciding what deserves an entry. 
14.4 Hyperlinked and Dynamic Indexes 


===== PAGE 30 =====
LIS 815 • INDEXING AND ABSTRACTING 
A Beginner-Friendly Guide to LIS 815 
30 
Electronic books, online textbooks, digital encyclopaedias, institutional repositories, and web-based 
publications are no longer restricted to static page numbers. Instead, they often use hyperlinks, 
bookmarks, anchors, and search functions in place of conventional locators. In a hyperlinked index, an 
entry links directly to the relevant section of the document, so a reader can click through to the required 
information immediately rather than manually turning to an indicated page. Some digital publications go 
further still, using dynamic indexes that update automatically whenever the underlying document is 
revised — unlike a printed index, which must be completely reworked after any change to pagination. 
Digital indexes may also link to figures, tables, appendices, multimedia resources such as audio and video, 
external websites, related chapters, and supplementary online materials, so that a modern digital index 
has evolved from a simple alphabetical list into an interactive navigation system. 
14.5 What Artificial Intelligence Can Do 
AI-powered systems apply Natural Language Processing (NLP), Machine Learning (ML), semantic analysis, 
and text mining to analyse documents and identify important concepts. In indexing and abstracting work 
more broadly, AI can assist with: 
• extracting keywords automatically; 
• identifying frequently occurring concepts; 
• recognising the names of people, organisations, and places (named-entity recognition); 
• detecting relationships among concepts and performing semantic indexing; 
• recommending indexing terms and generating preliminary index entries; 
• identifying synonyms and related expressions; 
• classifying topics according to subject categories; 
• suggesting appropriate cross-references; 
• generating metadata and assisting with automatic abstracting; and 
• supporting similarity detection, recommendation, and question-answering systems built on top of 
indexed collections. 
These capabilities substantially reduce the time required for preliminary indexing and abstracting, 
particularly when dealing with lengthy academic publications or very large digital collections. 
14.6 The Continuing Role of the Human Indexer 
Despite these advances, Artificial Intelligence has not replaced the professional indexer or abstractor. 
Indexing remains fundamentally an intellectual activity that requires human judgement, contextual 
understanding, subject expertise, and sensitivity to users’ information needs. AI systems can readily 
identify frequently occurring words, but they often struggle to determine which concepts are truly 
significant, meanings that are implied rather than stated, the difference between important and trivial 
information, the contextual meaning of an ambiguous term, the appropriate level of specificity, likely user 
search behaviour, and conceptual relationships that call for professional interpretation. 
EXAMPLE 
An AI system may notice the word “cloud” hundreds of times in a computer science textbook. A 
professional indexer recognises that the relevant concept is Cloud Computing, not simply “cloud,” and 
may divide the entry into subentries such as cloud infrastructure, cloud security, and cloud storage. 
Likewise, a history book may repeatedly mention “Washington” — a human indexer can tell whether 
this means George Washington, Washington, D.C., or Washington State, ensuring readers are 
directed accurately.

14.7 Benefits and Challenges of Digital and AI-Assisted Indexing 
The adoption of digital technologies has brought real benefits: faster index preparation, improved 
consistency of entries, automatic alphabetical arrangement, efficient management of cross-references, 
easier correction and revision, better collaboration between publishers and indexers across locations, 
support for very large indexing projects, interactive hyperlinks in electronic publications, reduced 
production costs, and improved accessibility through searchable, navigable indexes. 
These gains come with real challenges. AI-generated indexes may include irrelevant or excessive entries, 
because algorithms often rely on word frequency rather than genuine conceptual importance; automated 
systems may overlook implicit ideas, fail to recognise specialised terminology, or misinterpret context. 
Other recurring challenges include over-reliance on automated tools, inconsistencies in AI-generated 
terminology, software compatibility issues across publishing platforms, the need for continual software 
updates and staff training, difficulty indexing multimedia and interactive content, and ethical concerns 
relating to algorithmic bias and transparency. These limitations reinforce the importance of professional 
human oversight throughout the indexing and abstracting process. 
14.8 Future Trends 
The future of indexing and abstracting is expected to involve deeper integration of Artificial Intelligence, 
semantic technologies, and linked data: AI systems that understand context more accurately, semantic 
indexing that works with concepts rather than isolated keywords, multilingual indexing for global 
audiences, voice-search-compatible indexes, and personalised digital indexes that adapt to individual 
users’ interests and search histories. Cloud computing and collaborative online platforms are also 
enabling indexers, editors, and publishers to work simultaneously on the same projects regardless of 
location. As digital libraries and open-access repositories continue to expand, indexing and abstracting will 
increasingly support the discovery and retrieval of information across vast collections of interconnected 
resources — with the fundamental principles set out in this guide continuing to underpin that work, 
whichever tools are used to apply them.

Model Answers 
• Subject indexing represents the subject content of a document for the purpose of retrieval. 
• A controlled vocabulary is an organised set of authorised terms used consistently for indexing and 
retrieval. 
• BT is Broader Term, NT is Narrower Term, and RT is Related Term. 
• Pre-coordinate indexing combines concepts before retrieval takes place. 
• Post-coordinate indexing combines separate concepts during the act of retrieval. 
• KWIC is Keyword in Context; KWOC is Keyword Out of Context. 
• Exhaustivity concerns the number of important concepts represented; specificity concerns how 
precisely each one is represented. 
• Precision equals relevant retrieved divided by total retrieved. Recall equals relevant retrieved 
divided by total relevant. 
• Indicative abstracts describe the scope of a document; informative abstracts provide substantive 
information and findings. 
• CAS is a Current Awareness Service. 
• An index is an alphabetical guide to specific information within a document, usually placed at the 
end; a table of contents appears at the start and lists chapters in the order they occur. 
• PRECIS, the Preserved Context Index System, was developed by Derek Austin. 
• Automatic indexing uses computer algorithms to identify and assign index terms to a document with 
little or no manual intervention. 
• CINDEX, Sky Index, and Macrex are widely used computer-assisted indexing software packages. 
• A critical abstract summarises a document’s content while also offering an assessment of its 
strengths, weaknesses, or significance.

A Model Comparison Outline 
When answering a comparison question, such as one contrasting pre-coordinate and post-coordinate 
indexing, the following outline provides a reliable structure: 
1. Introduction: define both systems clearly. 
2. Timing: pre-coordinate combines concepts during indexing; post-coordinate combines them during 
retrieval. 
3. Roles: pre-coordinate places more responsibility on the indexer; post-coordinate gives the searcher 
more responsibility for combining concepts. 
4. Flexibility: post-coordinate generally permits more novel combinations of terms. 
5. Control: pre-coordinate can provide carefully designed, controlled conceptual expressions. 
6. Limitations: pre-coordinate indexing may fail to anticipate every combination a user might want; 
post-coordinate indexing depends heavily on the searcher’s own skill and use of terminology. 
7. Conclusion: the most suitable system depends on the particular information environment and 
retrieval objectives involved.

Appendix C: Glossary of Key Terms 
Abstract — A concise representation of a document’s content. 
Abstracting — The process of preparing abstracts. 
Assigned indexing — Indexing in which an indexer selects terms from an indexing language or controlled 
vocabulary, rather than copying them directly from the document. 
Author's abstract — An abstract prepared by the author of the original document. 
Automatic indexing — Indexing performed by computer algorithms with little or no manual intervention. 
BT — Broader Term; a more general concept within a thesaurus hierarchy. 
Book index — An alphabetical guide to the significant content of a book, with locators, usually placed at 
the end. 
CAS — Current Awareness Service. 
Chain indexing — A pre-coordinate indexing technique, associated with S. R. Ranganathan, that derives 
subject entries from the hierarchical chain of a classification scheme. 
Computer-assisted indexing — The use of software to support tasks such as sorting, formatting, and 
cross-reference management, while the indexer retains intellectual control. 
Controlled vocabulary — An organised set of authorised terms used consistently for indexing and 
retrieval. 
Critical abstract — An abstract that summarises a document while also offering an evaluation of its 
strengths, weaknesses, or significance. 
Cross-reference — A See or See also note directing a reader from one index term to another. 
Cyclic (cycled) indexing — A technique that varies the leading term in a compound subject to create 
multiple access points. 
Derived indexing — Indexing based on features taken directly from the document, such as title words. 
Dynamic index — A digital index that updates automatically when the underlying document changes. 
Exhaustivity — The extent to which important document concepts are represented in indexing. 
Fallout — The proportion of non-relevant documents retrieved, out of all non-relevant documents 
available. 
Hyperlinked index — A digital index whose entries link directly to the relevant section of a document. 
Index — A systematically arranged guide to the contents of a document, consisting of terms and locators. 
Indexing — Representing document content with terms or symbols to support retrieval. 
Indexing and abstracting service — A database or publication, such as Scopus or MEDLINE, that provides 
organised indexing and abstracting access to literature across a discipline. 
Indicative abstract — An abstract that indicates the scope or topics covered by a document. 
Informative abstract — An abstract that reports substantive content such as purpose, method, findings, 
and conclusion. 
ISO 999 / BS ISO 5963 — International and British standards providing guidance on the content, structure, 
and preparation of indexes. 
KWIC — Keyword in Context. 
KWOC — Keyword Out of Context. 
Locator — The page, section, or paragraph reference attached to an index entry. 
NT — Narrower Term; a more specific concept within a thesaurus hierarchy. 
Post-coordinate indexing — Indexing in which concepts are combined during retrieval.

Precision — The proportion of retrieved documents that are relevant.
