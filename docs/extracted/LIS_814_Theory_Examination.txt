Question 1   (Module 1 — Foundations and Types of Indexes) 
(a) Subject indexing is the process of representing what a document is about by assigning terms, 
phrases, symbols or other representations that a user can later search for to find the document. 
Subject analysis is the preceding intellectual stage in which the indexer examines the document to 
identify its main topic, significant concepts and their relationships, before any term is assigned. 
Scholarly definitions: Lancaster (2003) defines subject indexing as determining a document's 
subject content and assigning terms that adequately represent it for retrieval; Rowley and Farrow 
(2000) describe it as assigning descriptive terms representing a document's topics so they can be 
identified during searches; BS ISO 5963 and ISO 999 frame indexing as the systematic examination 
and representation of a document's intellectual content to facilitate retrieval. 
(b) Objectives (any four): accurate subject representation; consistent access points; efficient 
retrieval; reduction of ambiguity; connection of related concepts; support for user discovery; 
improved precision and recall. Guiding principles (any two): specificity (terms should be as precise 
as the vocabulary allows); consistency (the same concept always represented by the same term); 
user orientation (reflecting how users actually search); exhaustivity (all significant subjects 
represented); selectivity (only meaningful concepts indexed); objectivity (reflecting the 
document's actual content, not the indexer's opinion). 
(c) An index is a systematically arranged guide to a document's contents, consisting of headings, 
subheadings, locators and cross-references, usually placed at the end of a publication. Unlike a 
table of contents — which appears at the start, follows the book's own order, and lists chapters — 
an index is arranged alphabetically, lists subjects/names/places/concepts, and provides detailed 
access to specific information rather than showing structure. Stages of book indexing: reading the 
book; subject analysis; selection of entries; preparing main entries; preparing subentries; 
assigning locators; creating cross-references; alphabetical arrangement; editing; proofreading. 
Question 2   (Module 2 — Vocabulary Control) 
(a) A controlled vocabulary is an organised set of approved terms used consistently for indexing and 
retrieval; a thesaurus is a controlled vocabulary whose terms are connected by defined 
relationships (BT, NT, RT). Vocabulary control is necessary because it reduces unwanted variation 
in terminology and improves the match between the words an indexer assigns and the words a 
searcher later uses. 
(b) Example entry for DIGITAL LIBRARIES: BT — Libraries. NT — Institutional Repositories, Digital 
Archives (or similar narrower concepts). RT — Electronic Books, Information Technology. UF/USE 
— e.g. “E-Libraries USE Digital Libraries.” (Any reasonable, correctly-directed set of relationships 
should be credited.) 
(c) A scope note is a short statement clarifying how a term should be interpreted or applied within a 
vocabulary, useful where a term is ambiguous or has a specialised meaning. An alphabetical 
display arranges terms A–Z, convenient for a user who already knows the term they want; a 
classified display groups terms within a conceptual structure, supporting browsing. Maintenance 
tasks (any two): adding new terms; revising definitions/scope notes; merging or splitting 
concepts; updating cross-references; documenting decisions.

Question 3   (Module 3 — Pre-Coordinate Indexing Systems) 
(a) Pre-coordinate indexing combines the concepts of a subject into a single indexing expression at 
the time of indexing, before any search takes place, with the indexer deciding how concepts 
relate. Chain indexing derives subject entries from the hierarchical chain of concepts in a 
classification scheme; it is closely associated with S. R. Ranganathan. 
(b) Cyclic (cycled) indexing generates multiple index entries by varying the position or focus of terms 
within a compound subject, so each significant component in turn becomes the leading term, 
giving several access points to the same document. Example: “Artificial Intelligence — University 
Libraries — Nigeria” could also be cycled as “University Libraries — Artificial Intelligence — 
Nigeria” and “Nigeria — University Libraries — Artificial Intelligence.” 
(c) PRECIS stands for Preserved Context Index System, developed by Derek Austin for the British 
National Bibliography. It was designed to preserve the relationships between concepts in a 
subject statement while generating usable index entries, avoiding the ambiguity of an unordered 
list of keywords. Building a string: (1) understand the subject as a whole; (2) identify the individual 
concepts; (3) identify the role each concept plays; (4) apply the prescribed role operators; (5) 
generate the finished index string. 
Question 4   (Module 4 — Post-Coordinate and Derived Indexing) 
(a) Pre-coordinate indexing combines concepts during indexing, with the indexer responsible for the 
combination; post-coordinate indexing leaves concepts as separate terms that the searcher or 
system combines at the point of retrieval. A second difference: pre-coordinate systems offer 
more controlled, predictable access but may not anticipate every useful combination, while post-
coordinate systems are more flexible but place greater responsibility on the searcher. 
(b) Uniterm indexing assigns individual, single terms to documents and combines them only at 
retrieval — an early illustration of the post-coordinate principle; its limitation is that it depends 
heavily on the searcher combining terms correctly. Free-text searching looks for words directly in 
fields such as titles or abstracts without relying on a controlled vocabulary; its limitation is that it 
can suffer from unmatched synonyms, ambiguity and spelling variation. 
(c) Derived indexing obtains index terms directly from features of the document itself, most 
commonly the title, rather than from a controlled vocabulary. KWIC (Keyword in Context) displays 
a keyword together with its surrounding title words so context is preserved — e.g. indexing 
“Artificial Intelligence Applications in Nigerian University Libraries” under “Applications” would 
show it within its title context. KWOC (Keyword Out of Context) displays the same keyword as a 
separate heading above the full citation, outside the title. 
Question 5   (Module 5 — Evaluation and System Metrics) 
(a) Exhaustivity is the extent to which the important concepts in a document are represented by 
index terms; specificity is how precisely each indexing term represents the subject. The trade-off: 
greater exhaustivity gives more access points but, if indexing becomes too broad, precision can 
suffer through increased retrieval of irrelevant material. 
(b) The Cranfield research programme, associated with Cyril Cleverdon, established a systematic, 
evidence-based approach to evaluating retrieval systems using test collections, queries and

Question 6   (Module 6 — Abstracting Principles and Applications) 
(a) An abstract is a concise representation of a document's content, which may present its purpose, 
method, findings and conclusion. Qualities of a good abstract (any four): clear, concise, accurate, 
objective, self-contained, coherent, faithful to the source, free of unsupported claims or personal 
opinion. 
(b) An indicative abstract tells the reader what a document covers without detailed findings (e.g. an 
abstract simply describing that a paper “discusses the application of AI in university libraries”). An 
informative abstract reports substantive purpose, method, findings and conclusion (e.g. reporting 
the actual survey findings on AI adoption among Nigerian librarians). A critical abstract 
summarises the content while also evaluating its strengths, weaknesses or significance. A slant 
abstract emphasises the aspects of a document most relevant to a specific audience, such as 
librarians versus administrators reading the same source. 
(c) CAS (Current Awareness Services) keep users informed about newly available information in their 
subject area, often through alerts containing citations and abstracts. SDI (Selective Dissemination 
of Information) distributes newly available material according to each user's individual profile or 
interests. Major indexing and abstracting services (any two): Scopus, Web of Science, 
PubMed/MEDLINE, ERIC, Chemical Abstracts, LISA. 
Question 7   (Module 7 — Digital, Automated and AI-Assisted Indexing) 
(a) Automatic indexing uses computer algorithms to identify and assign index terms by analysing a 
document's titles, abstracts, full text, keywords, metadata and citations, without an indexer 
manually assigning every term. Advantages (any two): rapid processing; ability to handle large-
scale indexing; reduced manual labour; consistency of application. Limitations (any two): 
algorithmic misunderstanding of context; incorrect interpretation of ambiguous terms; missed 
semantic relationships; continued need for human quality control. 
(b) A hyperlinked index links each entry directly to the relevant section of a digital document, so a 
reader can click through immediately. A dynamic index goes further and updates automatically 
whenever the underlying document is revised, unlike a printed index which must be reworked 
after any change in pagination. Computer-assisted indexing software (any two): CINDEX, Sky 
Index, Macrex. 
(c) AI systems readily detect frequently occurring words but often cannot judge which concepts are 
genuinely significant, resolve ambiguous meanings, or select the right level of specificity. Example: 
an AI system may flag the word “cloud” hundreds of times in a computing text, but a human 
indexer recognises the relevant concept is Cloud Computing and can divide it into subentries such 
as cloud security and cloud storage; similarly, a human indexer can tell whether “Washington” 
refers to George Washington, Washington, D.C., or Washington State, while an algorithm may 
not.
